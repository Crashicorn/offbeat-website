First gigs & beyond

Become a DJ. Get your first gigs.

A practical route from first practice set to your first bookings: choose a lane, build a reliable set, make a simple portfolio and approach the right rooms.

By Offbeat Editorial TeamReviewed October 20268 min read

You don’t need a flagship controller, a record deal, or a personal brand before you can play a good set. You need a musical point of view, a dependable way to practise, and a small body of work that lets the right person hear what you do.

✳

First, pick the kind of work you want. Club, bar, mobile and wedding DJing overlap, but the music, equipment, preparation and booking conversations are different. You can change lanes later; choosing one now makes the next steps clearer.

A flexible roadmap

Build proof before you chase bookings

Use these as milestones, not a deadline. A reliable 20-minute set is a better signal than a calendar full of arbitrary practice hours.

  1. 01

    Make the first setup playable

    Borrow, use a controller you already own, or choose a modest two-channel setup. Confirm it connects to your headphones and speakers, then stop shopping and practise.

    Plan the whole rig, including cables →
  2. 02

    Make one short set you can stand behind

    Choose 10–15 tracks with a clear opening, a middle and a landing. Practise clean phrasing, cueing and levels; record a take and note one thing to fix.

    Learn transitions & phrasing →·Practise beatmatching by ear →
  3. 03

    Put together a small, honest portfolio

    Keep one 20–30-minute mix, a tracklist, a two-sentence bio and one current photo in a link you can send from your phone. Label the style and recording date; don’t pad it with effects or claim a room you haven’t played.

    Make a clean recording →
  4. 04

    Get in the room before asking for the slot

    Go to nights you genuinely like. Learn who books them, support other DJs, and ask about open decks or a short early slot when it fits. A specific, respectful note beats a bulk message.

A practice plan that fits a week

Four sessions. One useful recording.

Session 1 · Pick

Find 3 tracks that share a mood. Listen through; mark likely mix-in and mix-out points.

Session 2 · Blend

Practise the transition slowly. Keep the incoming bass low until you’re ready to trade.

Session 3 · Recover

Record a 15-minute run. When a mix drifts, correct it calmly instead of restarting.

Session 4 · Review

Listen back away from the decks. Write down one timing fix and one musical choice to keep.

Make the ask easy to answer

A short note beats an EPK nobody asked for

For a local club or bar, write to the actual booker or night organiser. Mention the event you mean, why your sound fits, the slot you’re asking about, and one listenable link. For a private event, ask about date, hours, audience, sound system, announcements and the run-of-show before quoting.

A useful first message

Subject: Support slot for [night] — [artist name]

Hi [name] — I’m a local [style] DJ and I’ve been coming to [night/venue]. The [specific detail] is close to the sets I’m building. Would you consider a 30-minute early slot on a future date? Here’s a recent 20-minute mix and tracklist: [link]. Happy to send a short bio or tech details if useful. Thanks, [name] · [phone/handle]

Make it true, specific and short. If you don’t hear back, one polite follow-up is enough; move on without turning a no or silence into a negotiation.

The part that protects the work

When a gig comes in, get the details straight

  • Confirm the brief: date, set length, arrival, music boundaries, audience, breaks and who makes the final call.
  • Confirm the booth: controller or club setup, software/media expectations, available inputs, monitors, microphone and who provides PA.
  • Agree money in writing: fee, deposit if applicable, travel, overtime, cancellation and payment date. There is no universal “correct” local rate; quote for time, preparation, equipment and responsibility.
  • Bring a backup: spare audio cables, power where appropriate, a second music copy and a plan if your laptop or controller fails.
  • Know the rights question: ask the venue or client who handles public-performance licensing and any recording or livestream permissions. Don’t assume an online music subscription covers a public event.

For weddings and mobile events, the client experience starts long before the first track. Use a timeline, collect pronunciation and announcement notes, test the room and agree what happens when requests clash with the brief.

Questions people ask before their first gig

How long does it take to become a DJ?

There’s no reliable deadline. You can learn basic transitions in weeks of focused practice, but booking confidence depends on the music, the room and the responsibility of the event. Let your recorded sets and live readiness set the pace.

Do I have to produce music to get booked?

No. Production is one route to an audience, not an entry requirement. A well-curated set, dependable communication and a good fit for a night can matter more for many local bookings.

Should I play for free?

There is no single rule. A short, clearly scoped open-decks slot or community event can be useful if you want the experience. Don’t accept vague unpaid work that expects professional equipment, travel and a full night without clear terms.

Do I need a press kit?

Start with a mix, tracklist, short bio and reachable contact. Build a fuller press kit only when venues, clients or festivals ask for more.
