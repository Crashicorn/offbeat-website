A DJ setup that fits how you actually play

Four questions, one honest answer: what to buy first, what to leave until later, and where your money is actually going. No account, no email, nothing stored.

Most "what should I buy" advice fails for the same reason: it answers a question you did not ask. A bedroom beginner, a wedding DJ and someone preparing for club slots need different things from the same budget, and the order they buy them in matters more than the models they pick.

This planner asks what your rig has to do, then builds the smallest setup that does it. It is deliberately conservative — it will tell you to keep saving, or to buy headphones before hardware, when that is the right answer.

How the planner decides

There is no machine learning here — just the rules a working DJ would apply, written down. They are worth knowing even if you ignore the output.

RuleWhat it means in practice
Software before hardwareThe controller you buy decides which software you can run, and that is the decision that is hardest to undo. Choose the ecosystem, then the box.
The controller is about half the budgetA $400 rig is a $220 controller, $70 headphones, $60 of cables and adapters, and a stand. People budget for the deck and then discover the rest.
Never buy a rig you cannot listen toIf headphones are missing, they come before a bigger controller. You cannot learn to mix without a cue mix.
Buy the format you will play inClub-bound? A club-format layout. Wedding work? Reliability and a mic input. Streaming? A clean audio path.
Standalone needs a reasonRemoving the laptop is convenience, not capability. It costs roughly three times as much and only pays back if you are playing out regularly.
Leave room to upgradeNothing here is the last thing you will buy. The planner says what to change first so you are not replacing the whole rig at once.

Where the money actually goes

What the planner will not do

  • Quote live prices. Street prices move weekly and bundles change constantly. Every band here links to a dated guide that carries the researched figure and the date it was checked.
  • Value used gear precisely. The second-hand market is where the best value sits, and also where the worst decisions happen. Our budget guide covers what to check before buying used.
  • Decide for you. It gives you the shape of the rig and the order to buy in. The specific model is a judgement call, and the linked reviews explain the trade-offs.

Frequently asked questions

How much should a first DJ setup cost?

Plan on $400–$700 for a working rig: a two-channel controller, closed-back headphones, cables and a stand. The controller is roughly half of that. Under $300 total usually means buying used or delaying the speakers.

Should I buy a controller or a standalone system?

A controller, unless you are already playing paid gigs regularly. Standalone gear starts around $900 and removes the laptop, which is convenience rather than capability.

Do I need speakers to start?

Not immediately — headphones and a controller are enough to learn. You do need speakers to learn how a mix sounds in a room, so plan for them within the first few months.

Does the planner store my answers?

No. It runs entirely in your browser, makes no network requests and stores nothing. Answers are only encoded in the page URL so a result can be bookmarked or shared.
